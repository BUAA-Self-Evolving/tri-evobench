Large language models can infer sensitive attributes from distributed semantic and contextual cues in released text, even after explicit identifiers have been removed. Existing anonymization methods often treat privacy-relevant evidence uniformly, which can leave residual inference pathways or unnecessarily suppress content needed to preserve the user's communicative intent. We present \textsc{IntentAnony}, an intent-constrained exposure-control framework that links pragmatic intent to attribute-specific privacy evidence and regulates the inferential support retained in released text under explicit policy constraints. Before release, candidate rewrites are further verified for residual inference risk and intent consistency. Crucially, intent relevance serves solely as a utility constraint: it neither authorizes disclosure nor changes the privacy status of successful attribute recovery. On Personal Reddit and SynthPAI, \textsc{IntentAnony} achieves favorable privacy--utility trade-offs over masking, paraphrasing, and LLM-based anonymization baselines on curated non-intent targets, while semantic and intent-preservation evaluations indicate stronger communicative utility. Cross-model evaluation nevertheless reveals substantial attacker- and dataset-dependent variation, underscoring persistent residual inference risk. These results demonstrate the empirical value of intent-constrained exposure control under the evaluated settings, without implying protection for intent-relevant attributes or any formal privacy guarantee.
